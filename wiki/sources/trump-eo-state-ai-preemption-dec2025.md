## Précis

Signed December 11, 2025, this executive order is the Trump second term's most direct structural intervention against state-level AI governance. It declares a federal policy of "minimally burdensome national standard" for AI and creates four enforcement mechanisms: (1) an AI Litigation Task Force within the DOJ, to be established within 30 days, whose sole mandate is to challenge state AI laws on Commerce Clause, preemption, or other grounds; (2) a Commerce Department evaluation of state AI laws within 90 days identifying those that conflict with the order's policy, with particular attention to laws requiring AI models to "alter their truthful outputs" or compel disclosure in ways that would violate the First Amendment; (3) BEAD broadband program funding conditions — states with identified "onerous AI laws" become ineligible for non-deployment BEAD funds; and (4) a directive to FTC to assert FTC Act preemption over state laws requiring "deceptive conduct" in AI models (i.e., laws mandating changes to "truthful outputs" are characterized as requiring deception, inverting the normal regulatory framing).

The order carves out four categories of state law it explicitly does not seek to preempt: child safety protections, AI compute and data center infrastructure (except broadly applicable permitting), state government procurement and use of AI, and unspecified other topics. It directs a legislative recommendation for a uniform federal framework, and involves the FCC in a proceeding to determine whether to adopt a federal AI reporting and disclosure standard that would preempt conflicting state laws.

The practical effect — targeting Colorado's "algorithmic discrimination" law as the motivating example — is to characterize anti-discrimination requirements in AI as both ideologically biased and unconstitutionally intrusive on interstate commerce. This is a legally aggressive framing: calling a disparate-impact standard a mandate for "false results" is a contested legal claim. The order's combined use of spending conditions (BEAD), litigation threat (DOJ Task Force), and FTC preemption theory creates a multi-vector pressure campaign against state AI regulation, extending well beyond the earlier White House letter killing the Utah chatbot bill.

## Key terms introduced

- AI Litigation Task Force — DOJ unit created within 30 days to challenge state AI laws
- BEAD funding conditionality — novel mechanism using broadband deployment funds as leverage over state AI policy

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

- This order extends and formalizes the pattern documented in [[wiki/findings/white-house-preemption-pressure-state-ai|White House Preemption Pressure]], where informal executive pressure killed the Utah chatbot bill; here the mechanism is formalized into litigation and spending levers.
- The carve-out for child safety is notable given the Connecticut SB 5 AI companion framework — those provisions would likely be protected. The carve-out for state government procurement is consistent with a pattern of leaving states' own use of AI relatively ungoverned.
- The order creates a direct conflict with the EU's approach of requiring AI systems to be correctable for bias — what Brussels requires, Washington would now litigate against in any U.S. state that tried to implement similar requirements.
