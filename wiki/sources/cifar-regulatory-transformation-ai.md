## Précis

This CIFAR policy brief (Sandhu, Kolt & Hadfield, November 2023) argues that the prevailing approach to AI regulation — which it names the **harms paradigm** — is necessary but incomplete. The harms paradigm focuses on identifying and mitigating risks posed by AI systems: this characterizes the EU AI Act (risk tiers with corresponding requirements), Canada's AIDA (high-impact system obligations), and the NIST AI RMF (enterprise risk management). The critique is not that harms matter, but that this framing misses a more fundamental challenge: as a general-purpose technology, AI has the potential to transform the regulatory system itself, challenging both the *targets* of regulation (the entities and actors regulation is designed to govern) and the *tools* of regulation (the mechanisms used to govern them).

The paper develops this through two Canadian case studies and a proposed framework. In **healthcare**, regulation has traditionally targeted human actors (doctors, nurses) and medical devices. AI tools like diagnostic LLMs cut across professions and blur the device/professional line — creating questions regulators are structurally unequipped to answer: Who undergoes training requirements, the human specialist or the software engineer? How is liability allocated across the causal chain? Medical device classification regimes designed for narrow-purpose Class I–IV hardware cannot govern general-purpose medical AIs that are deployed dynamically across contexts. In **financial services**, traditional regulatory tools — mandatory disclosure, auditing, professional licensing — were designed for human actors at identifiable firms. AI transforms this in two directions: individually, by enabling robo-advisors and AI-driven investment services that may not fit fiduciary frameworks; systemically, by enabling correlated AI behavior (homogeneous AI-generated investment decisions) and autonomous AI agents that could destabilize markets in ways the individual-harm focus of current frameworks cannot address.

The paper's constructive contribution is the **Regulatory Impacts Analysis (RIA)** framework: a 15-question structured questionnaire organized into three sections — *Regulatory Targets* (questions 1–5: who is currently regulated, how AI changes that), *Regulatory Tools* (questions 6–10: what mechanisms are used, how AI changes them), and *Next Steps* (questions 11–15: which targets become more/less important, which tools need development, and what resources are required). The nuclear energy case study (CNSC) demonstrates RIA in practice: the AI-expanded threat surface (AI-enabled cyber operations targeting nuclear facilities) changes both targets (new malicious actor types) and tools (cybersecurity monitoring). RIA is designed for periodic use — not a one-time audit — because AI's regulatory impacts will evolve.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The "harms paradigm" critique directly targets the regulatory frameworks most prominently represented in the wiki: [[wiki/sources/eu-ai-act-overview|EU AI Act]] and [[wiki/sources/nist-ai-rmf|NIST AI RMF]] are paradigm examples of what the paper argues is incomplete. This does not mean those frameworks are wrong — the harms paradigm addresses real harms — but that they are insufficient. The RIA framework is more actionable and sector-specific than Hadfield's regulatory markets model (which is more architecturally radical) and more practical than the IVO framework (which addresses a specific governance mechanism). The compute-governance framing in [[wiki/sources/computing-power-ai-governance|Sastry et al.]] is implicitly a regulatory instruments argument — the paper's distinction between tools and targets provides a vocabulary for locating compute governance on the "tools" side of the ledger.
