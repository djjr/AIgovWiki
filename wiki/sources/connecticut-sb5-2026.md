# Connecticut SB 5 (2026) — AI Responsibility and Transparency Act

## Précis

Connecticut SB 5 — formally "An Act Concerning Online Safety," renamed the Connecticut Artificial Intelligence Responsibility and Transparency Act on passage — was approved by the House 131-17 and awaits Gov. Lamont's signature (as of 2026-05-02). It is the first enacted U.S. state law to combine AI companion regulation, frontier model whistleblower protection, and algorithmic employment disclosure in a single omnibus statute, effective October 1, 2026, with the companion provisions effective January 1, 2027.

The bill is notable for what it dropped as much as what it contains. Three years of comprehensive anti-discrimination bills modeled on employment law failed; this version narrows to disclosure requirements and safety design mandates, with accountability provisions progressively traded away in negotiation. Most consequentially, the requirement to provide a "high-level statement" explaining adverse employment decisions was removed at the governor's request — leaving a disclosure-only AEDT framework that tells workers an algorithm was used but gives them no basis to contest the decision.

The six substantive regulatory pillars are: (1) AI subscription disclosure (§1): written notice of key terms before entering or renewing paid AI subscriptions, enforceable as CUTPA violations; (2) frontier developer whistleblower protection (§2): anti-retaliation for employees reporting catastrophic risks, with anonymous internal reporting channels required at large frontier developers (>$500M revenue) by January 1, 2027; (3) AI regulatory sandbox (§3): DECD to develop a plan, with multistate reciprocity assessed, by January 1, 2028 (effective July 1, 2027); (4) AI companion safety (§§4-6): a comprehensive framework distinguishing all-user disclosure requirements from heightened prohibitions on manipulation of minors; (5) automated employment decision technology disclosure (§§7-14): notice when AEDT is a "substantial factor," antidiscrimination law preserved, explanation requirement removed; and (6) independent verification pilot (§33): up to five DCP-approved third-party verifiers to assess AI and privacy law compliance.

Two features deserve particular attention. First, CT SB 5 is the first U.S. state statute to define "frontier developer" using a compute threshold — 10²⁶ FLOPs, matching the EU AI Act GPAI figure — making it the inaugural American legislative adoption of compute-based jurisdiction over frontier AI. Second, the "covered employee" definition in §2 explicitly includes employees responsible for addressing a "deceptive technique" used by a foundation model against its own developer — the first legislative encoding of deceptive alignment as a safety-relevant risk category.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

- The IVO pilot (§33) is the first real-world test of the [[wiki/definitions/regulatory-markets|Regulatory Markets]] model Hadfield proposes, but is limited — no liability shield, no competition among verifiers for business, no new substantive standards to verify against. It operationalizes the structural form while omitting the incentive mechanisms that make Hadfield's model self-sustaining.
- The AEDT disclosure-only approach exemplifies the [[wiki/definitions/scrutability|Scrutability]] problem and the [[wiki/definitions/level-1-level-2-outcomes|Level 1 / Level 2 Outcomes]] gap: the Level 1 goal (no discriminatory employment decisions) is nominally preserved, but the Level 2 enforcement mechanism (explanations that enable contestation) was traded away.
- The compute-based frontier developer definition (10²⁶ FLOPs) aligns CT with the EU AI Act threshold but potentially conflicts with any future federal definition — a preemption landmine if Washington adopts a different figure.
