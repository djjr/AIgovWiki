## Précis

Nathalie Smuha's *Algorithmic Rule by Law* (Cambridge University Press) examines a problem that sits at the intersection of two largely siloed literatures: scholarship on the erosion of the rule of law in EU liberal democracies, and scholarship on the risks of algorithmic regulation by public authorities. Smuha's central argument is that algorithmic regulation — defined narrowly as public authorities' use of algorithmic systems to inform or take administrative acts — can hollow out the law's protective function while preserving its formal veneer of legitimacy, a threat she conceptualizes as "algorithmic rule by law." This is distinct from mere individual-rights violations (which the EU AI Act addresses) and from explicitly authoritarian algorithmic governance (which is well-studied). The distinctive danger is incremental, opaque erosion of constitutional checks within liberal democracies that think of themselves as rule-of-law states.

The chapter identifies a structural schizophrenia in the EU: its rule-of-law agenda (monitoring and sanctioning Member States that erode judicial independence, equality, and separation of powers) is in direct tension with its digital agenda (actively funding and requiring algorithmic regulation by those same Member States' executive branches). Smuha argues that the EU AI Act, while a serious effort, fails to close this gap because it focuses primarily on individual and collective harm and treats societal harm — harm to the rule of law as a constitutional value held by society at large — as largely out of scope. The Act also relies heavily on self-certification by AI providers, a mechanism she finds structurally inadequate given the opacity of algorithmic systems. The chapter introduces a three-tier harm taxonomy (individual, collective, societal) that is analytically important: legal mechanisms designed to protect against the first two do not necessarily protect against the third.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The harms paradigm critique ([[wiki/findings/harms-paradigm-critique-ai-regulation|Sandhu/Kolt/Hadfield]]) focuses on AI's challenge to *regulatory tools and targets* — a different angle from Smuha's focus on AI's challenge to the *constitutional order*. These are complementary, not contradictory, but Smuha's analysis is more specifically EU-law-grounded and more focused on executive-branch algorithmic deployment than on commercial AI. Smuha's critique of the AI Act's self-certification reliance reinforces the FMTI finding on declining developer transparency ([[wiki/findings/frontier-model-transparency-declining|Frontier Model Transparency Is Declining]]).
