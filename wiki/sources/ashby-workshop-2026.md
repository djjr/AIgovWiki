# 2026 Ashby Workshops: Designing Our Collective AI Future

## Précis

The 2026 Ashby Workshops — presented by Fathom, February 2–4, 2026, Middleburg, Virginia — was a curated cross-sector convening bringing together frontier lab representatives (Kevin Troy/Anthropic, Zico Kolter/CMU-OpenAI, Owen Larter/Google DeepMind), former White House AI officials from both parties (Ben Buchanan/Biden, Dean Ball/Trump), national security experts, faith leaders, consumer advocates, educators, healthcare providers, and state policymakers. Non-mainstage sessions were conducted under Chatham House Rule; this report reflects the attributed mainstage panels and synthesized workshop themes.

The report's organizing question — "Which societal-level challenges uniquely raised by AI will require our greatest collective action and energy to solve?" — was confronted across ten workshops and four mainstage panels. Four common threads emerged across all discussions: (1) labor market uncertainty is now a governing reality (no consensus on timeline or scale of displacement; disagreement cut across partisan, disciplinary, and sectoral lines); (2) institutional adaptation, not technical capability, is now the dominant constraint on responsible AI deployment; (3) delay is a choice with consequences — the window for shaping governance and market norms will not remain open indefinitely; (4) adoption requires trust, and trust requires infrastructure — the verification systems every other high-stakes industry has built over time do not yet exist for AI.

Five priority challenges structured Fathom's framing: (1) Consumer and Enterprise Trust & Agency — deployment will stall not because the technology doesn't work but because there is no credible way to demonstrate that it does; (2) Well-Managed Worker Transition — existing support structures (unemployment insurance, reskilling) were not designed for this moment; (3) Plans for AGI & Loss of Control — frontier labs have published preparedness frameworks, but these are self-imposed guardrails; institutional capacity for independent oversight is missing precisely where stakes are highest; (4) Resources Directed Toward Maximizing Good — market logic misaligns investment toward quick-scaling applications rather than public goods; (5) Human-Centered AI — efficiency metrics inadequately capture the agency, creativity, and meaning at stake.

The "Trust Infrastructure" section (pp. 10) is the most operationally significant for this wiki. Ashby's explicit identification of trust as the central variable maps directly onto the IVO model: "The prospect of an independent oversight marketplace for AI is gaining momentum as the best solution to build a trust infrastructure in the AI era." A new incentive mechanism beyond tort law appears: insurance markets — "as insurers begin pricing AI risk, verified systems are likely to earn lower premiums, giving developers a concrete financial incentive to seek certification rather than avoid it." The three-tier diagram (Government → Independent Evaluators → The Market) is Fathom's clearest public articulation of the regulatory markets model.

Key named panelist moments: Kevin Troy (Anthropic) — "I think we could do more to try to convey to the public the inside view of how crazy fast this feels." Zico Kolter (CMU/OpenAI) — "There is an opportunity for people of a variety of different backgrounds to actually set conditions and policy in a way that can have a massive impact on how these systems are deployed." The Workforce Debate panel surfaced that at Anthropic, 100% of coding is now done with Claude, up from 40–60% five months prior — the starkest quantitative deployment data point in the report.

The US-China simulation (workshop: "Competition to Cooperation: Planning for a U.S.-China AI Accord") produced an insight worth indexing: "Moments of American technological advantage may paradoxically be the best opportunities for diplomacy, since they create the conditions under which China has the most to gain from coming to the table."

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

- The insurance market incentive mechanism (lower premiums for IVO-certified systems) is new and complements the tort law rebuttable presumption mechanism from Hadfield's model. Together they suggest multiple independent market incentive pathways for IVO participation — which matters for the adverse selection concern in [[wiki/findings/voluntary-ivo-design-tradeoff|Voluntary IVO Participation: Design Tradeoff]].
- The "institutional adaptation as dominant constraint" framing partially contradicts the TIAP 2026 "governance infrastructure gap" framing: TIAP emphasizes the gap between governance tools and what policymakers need; Ashby emphasizes the gap between existing institutions and what governance requires. These are complementary, not contradictory — but the emphasis differs (TIAP: tools problem; Ashby: institutions problem).
- The AGI/loss of control section (p. 5) directly echoes China's Framework 2.0 Principle 1.5 (防范失控) and the Singapore Consensus. Ashby's framing — "as they grow more capable and more autonomous, we cannot assume they will consistently act in accordance with human intent" — is nominally convergent with the international safety community framing. The concern about "self-imposed guardrails" at frontier labs echoes the mandatory audit call in Ball & Buchanan (May 2026).
- The children's safety workshop (p. 9) reached an uncomfortable conclusion that links to CT SB 5's companion AI framework: "The most dangerous AI interactions children will have are the ones no rating system will anticipate, and no regulator will see in time." This challenges the sufficiency of CT SB 5's enumerated prohibitions — which presuppose that dangerous interactions are categorizable in advance.
