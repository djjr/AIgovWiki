## Précis

JD Supra comparative analysis (2026) of AI content provenance statutes in Utah (Digital Content Provenance Standards Act, eff. January 1, 2027), Washington (HB 1170, eff. February 1, 2027), and California (eff. August 2, 2026). All three require "covered providers" — GenAI systems with more than one million monthly users publicly accessible within the state — to embed latent disclosures or provenance metadata in AI-generated or substantially modified image, video, and audio content, using widely accepted technical standards. The analysis reveals that while the nominal obligation is similar across all three, the statutes diverge substantially in three dimensions that determine their practical reach.

First, **platform-layer obligations**: Utah and California impose additional duties on large online platforms (>2 million monthly users) to detect provenance data in distributed content and present it to users via a disclosure interface; Washington focuses exclusively on covered providers and imposes no distinct platform-tier requirements. Second, **capture device manufacturers**: Utah (from 1/1/28) and California (from 1/1/28) require device manufacturers to embed latent disclosures in captured content; Washington has no such provision. Third, **enforcement**: Utah relies on Division of Consumer Protection administrative fines (up to $2,500/violation, $5,000 for order violations); Washington empowers only the Attorney General — no private right of action, violations are per se unfair trade practices; California authorizes the AG, city attorneys, and county counsels with civil penalties of $5,000 per violation per day.

Together with [[wiki/findings/connecticut-sb5-content-provenance|CT SB 5 §15]] (already enacted), these three statutes form an emerging four-state content provenance framework that represents the most significant U.S. development in AI-generated content accountability law to date.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The proliferation of state content provenance laws creates a compliance fragmentation problem the existing wiki has not yet addressed: covered providers must simultaneously satisfy four different technical standards regimes (CT, UT, WA, CA), with different UI obligations, device manufacturer duties, and enforcement risks. This is structurally analogous to the pre-GDPR EU member-state data protection fragmentation and has similar implications for whether a federal floor would help or hinder.
