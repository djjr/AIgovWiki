## Précis

This Lawfare essay (2026) provides the most detailed legal anatomy of the Anthropic-Pentagon-OpenAI standoff, moving from the high-level structural argument of the HKS/Carr Center commentary to the specific contractual and statutory mechanisms at stake. The author argues that the U.S. has moved toward **regulation by contract**: AI governance rules are no longer derived from statutes or regulations but from bilateral agreements between the government and individual vendors — agreements that bind only the parties who signed them and that most affected populations had no role in shaping. The crisis has a specific origin: the January 2026 Hegseth AI strategy memo, which directed that all Defense Department AI contracts within 180 days must include "any lawful use" language requiring removal of vendor restrictions beyond what law mandates. When Anthropic refused, the Pentagon designated it a supply-chain risk; within hours OpenAI signed a deal.

The essay's legal analysis is granular and consequential. Most deals are executed as **Other Transaction (OT) agreements** outside the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), meaning they lack FAR's default contract-clause protections; guardrails exist only if the parties negotiated them in. Two specific drafting problems in the OpenAI agreement are identified: (1) the **"consistent with applicable laws" preamble** shifts interpretive authority to the government — when the Pentagon is the first and often only interpreter of what is lawful, and the contractor's remedies are post-hoc, this is effectively a permission structure, not a prohibition; (2) the **"intentionally" qualifier** on domestic surveillance narrows the prohibition so that incidental collection of person-specific information during a broader task may not be covered. The essay's deepest point is that even robust contractual prohibitions cannot function as governance: the vendor's only real remedy — termination — arrives after the harm has occurred, and in practice the national security context may render even termination unavailable. Procurement was never designed to carry questions of constitutional rights, autonomous weapons policy, and intelligence oversight. These require public law, congressional action, and judicial review.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

Complements [[wiki/sources/governance-by-procurement|the HKS/Carr Center essay]] but at a different register: where the Carr Center piece argues from democratic theory and human rights universalism, this essay argues from contract law and procurement mechanics. Together they bracket the failure from both normative and technical sides. The "safety stack" as the real enforcement mechanism (OpenAI retains cloud-only deployment with classifiers) creates an unresolved tension with the Hegseth memo's instruction to use "models free from usage policy constraints" — if the safety stack blocks a lawful use, which provision controls? Altman's own words ("I believe we will hopefully have the best models that will encourage the government to be willing to work with us, *even if our safety stack annoys them*") are cited as the most honest assessment of the structural problem. This parallels the voluntary governance failure documented in the RSP literature: safety commitments hold only as long as the competitive environment tolerates them.
