## Précis

This OECD report (approved by the Public Governance Committee, September 2025) analyzes the current state and way forward for AI adoption *within* government — "governing with AI" as distinct from governing AI as a regulatory object. It draws on 200 use cases across 11 core government functions and extensive member-country consultation. This is a distinct analytical angle from most of the wiki's sources, which focus on regulatory frameworks for the private sector; this report focuses on whether and how governments are actually deploying AI in their own operations.

The report's central empirical finding is that **government AI adoption substantially lags the private sector** and is structurally disadvantaged relative to it: governments face unique headwinds including skills shortages, outdated legacy systems, data availability constraints, and higher requirements around privacy, transparency, and representational fairness. Most government AI efforts remain in exploratory or pilot phases, with limited documentation and scaling. The Alan Turing Institute estimates AI could automate 84% of repetitive public service transactions in the UK, saving 1,200 person-years of work annually — a potential that is far from realized. A crucial framing: failure to adopt AI is also a risk, not just a safe default. Governments that fail to build internal AI capacity become technology-takers rather than option-shapers, widening the capability gap with the private sector and losing citizen trust.

In terms of where AI is being used, the report finds it most prevalent in public service delivery and justice administration (which face urgent backlogs), and least prominent in government oversight and policymaking. GenAI/LLMs are significantly less common than rules-based and classical ML approaches — a notable gap given the capabilities discussion elsewhere in the wiki. The benefits most sought are automated and streamlined processes, followed by better decision-making and enhanced anomaly detection. Almost no use cases aim to unlock new opportunities for citizens or external stakeholders.

The report proposes a three-part framework for trustworthy government AI: **Enablers** (governance, data, digital infrastructure, skills, financial investment, procurement agility); **Guardrails** (rules, frameworks, transparency, accountability mechanisms, oversight bodies across the AI lifecycle); and **Engagement** (user-centred design, civil society input, public communication). This is a practical operational framework for government CIOs and digital leaders, not a theoretical model, and it reflects the OECD's positioning as a capacity-building body for member governments.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The "governing with AI" frame is almost entirely absent from the rest of the wiki, which is focused on AI regulation/governance as policy for AI companies. This report adds the dimension of AI as a governance tool within the state, which connects to [[wiki/sources/cifar-regulatory-transformation-ai|Sandhu, Kolt & Hadfield's]] argument that AI changes regulatory tools — an argument that applies with particular force when government agencies are themselves the deployers. The Enablers/Guardrails/Engagement framework is structurally similar to the Singapore Consensus's three-pillar defence-in-depth model, suggesting some convergence in governance architecture across different institutional contexts. The report's finding that GenAI is rare in government AI use cases creates a notable gap with the [[wiki/sources/stanford-hai-ai-index-2026-policy|Stanford HAI AI Index]] and other sources that focus heavily on LLMs.
