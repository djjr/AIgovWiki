# AI Laws and Regulation in China (CMS Law, Feb 2026)

## Précis

This CMS Law jurisdiction guide (updated February 2026) provides the most comprehensive English-language survey of China's AI regulatory landscape available in this wiki. Its core finding is the structural contrast with western approaches: China has no comprehensive AI statute (unlike the EU AI Act or proposed US federal bills) and no current timeline for one. Legislation has been on the Standing Committee of the NPC's agenda since 2023, but as of December 2025, no official draft had been released.

Instead, China governs AI through a stack of sector-specific regulations administered primarily by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC): the *Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services* (GenAI Measures, the most important), *Administrative Provisions on Deep Synthesis in Internet-based Information Services* (deepfakes), *Administrative Provisions on Recommendation Algorithms* (algorithmic feeds), and new content labeling rules. These are supplemented by a growing body of mandatory national technical standards (GB/T and GB series), several of which directly adopt ISO/IEC international standards, making China's national standards partly interoperable with international norms despite the lack of direct reference in legislation.

The CAC is the lead AI regulator, coordinating with MIIT (industry development and standards), Ministry of Science and Technology (ethics), SAMR (standardization), and sector-specific authorities. China is assessed as "medium" risk by CMS — active enforcement, aggressive guidance-issuance, and an expanding list of registered generative AI services requiring pre-launch review. The regulatory approach is characterized by short-cycle rulemaking (measures rather than statutes), rapid updates as technology evolves, and enforcement campaigns targeting specific risk categories (AI misuse, deepfakes, content violations).

The document notes China's 2023 *Global AI Governance Initiative* and ongoing participation in international AI standards bodies as signals of its interest in shaping international norms, alongside its domestic regulatory structure.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

- China's sectoral model (many specific rules, no comprehensive statute) contrasts sharply with the EU's risk-based comprehensive statute and the US's current deregulatory posture — three genuinely distinct governance models now on record, none converging.
- China's adoption of ISO/IEC standards into national GB/T standards provides a formal channel for international norm diffusion that coexists with explicit rejection of western regulatory frameworks — "converging in practice, diverging in principle" is the key characterization.
- The absence of a comprehensive AI statute is not the same as the absence of governance; CMS's "medium risk" rating for China reflects active, fast-moving sector-specific regulation that may in practice be more constraining than a comprehensive statute with long implementation timelines.
