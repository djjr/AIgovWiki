## Précis

This is a lightly edited transcript of Rob Reich's talk at FAR.AI Labs in March 2025. Reich served as one of the first five employees at the US AI Safety Institute (AISI) from April 2024 through January 17, 2025 (the day before the inauguration), on loan from Stanford. The talk covers AISI's origins, its three pillars of work, its institutional design decisions, and Reich's broader views on AI governance as a field.

On **AISI's institutional context**: the institute was never congressionally authorized or funded, operated on "couch change" from the Department of Commerce budget, and grew from a voluntary commitment framework pre-negotiated by the Biden White House with frontier AI companies in spring 2023. Its three pillars are: (1) advancing the science of AI safety — developing empirically sound, replicable capability and harm evaluations as a genuine scientific practice rather than ad-hoc red-teaming; (2) developing and administering the tests and evaluations under the voluntary commitment framework; (3) domestic and global dissemination of AI safety practices, culminating in the November 2024 launch of the international network of AI safety institutes (initially 10 government members including EU, Singapore, Japan, Canada, France, UK, Kenya). Reich reports that as of his departure, no company had left the voluntary framework and most staff remained.

Reich's most distinctive intellectual contribution is his **professionalization argument**: AI governance requires working on two parallel tracks — law, policy, and regulation on one hand; and professional norms, standards, and responsibilities emerging from within the field on the other. A statute requiring digital watermarking is inert without professional standards for what watermarking means technically. AIML, he argues bluntly, is a **professionally immature discipline** — it has existed formally for only ~70 years (CS) and ~25 years in its current powerful form, and its internal professional norms are thin compared to biomedical research or law. The CRISPR-Cas9 parallel is instructive: after Jennifer Doudna's Hitler nightmare, leading biomedical researchers called for a voluntary moratorium on human embryo editing within months of the technology's discovery. When He Jiankui violated the norm, he was excommunicated from global science — no conference, no journals, eventually imprisoned. Reich challenges his audience: "Can you think of an AIML developer who didn't break the law but violated a widely understood norm and suffered a professional cost for it?" The answer's absence diagnoses the problem.

On **China and IAISR design**: Reich identifies a genuine design tension — whether to anchor the international network in democracy and human rights (counseling against China's inclusion) versus anchoring in the science of AI safety and open-science norms (counseling for inclusion). He favors a middle path that insulates safety institutes from partisan and geopolitical politics while acknowledging full insulation is impossible.

On **policy change models**: Reich distinguishes incremental policy change (the current trajectory — GDPR, EU AI Act, AISIs as a decade-long policy window in year 2-3) from catastrophe-triggered change (911 → Patriot Act; Tuskegee → institutional review boards). He notes it is not logically necessary that a catastrophe trigger policy change, but it is a historical pattern, and argues the AI safety community should be developing "off-the-shelf" dramatic policy responses ready for immediate deployment after a range of AI catastrophe scenarios — a task far fewer people are working on than incremental governance.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

Reich's assessment that AISI was in good institutional health as of January 2025 (no company departures, no deliberate cuts) was forward-looking; the January 2025 political transition introduced real uncertainty. The voluntary commitment framework's durability is consistent with [[wiki/sources/anthropic-rsp|Anthropic RSP]] and [[wiki/sources/openai-preparedness-framework|OpenAI Preparedness]] as working examples, but the February 2026 Anthropic-Pentagon standoff (detailed in [[wiki/sources/governance-by-procurement|governance-by-procurement]] and [[wiki/sources/military-ai-policy-by-contract|military-ai-policy-by-contract]]) shows exactly the kind of structural failure Reich's analysis predicts: voluntary commitments are incentive-compatible only until competitive and political pressures shift. Reich's catastrophe-trigger policy change argument is the most explicit treatment in the wiki of the "ready-on-the-shelf" regulatory design problem.
