# Connecticut SB 5 (2026) — Senate Passage and Employer Analysis

## Précis

Two accounts cover events prior to the House passage we have already processed: the CT Mirror's April 21, 2026 report on the Senate's 32-4 vote, and the CBIA (Connecticut Business & Industry Association)'s post-Senate employer-facing analysis.

The CT Mirror Senate article adds the crucial political framing that the House vote accounts omit: the bill passed the Senate only after a strike-all amendment was introduced the same afternoon — meaning most senators voted on a 70+ page bill they had had hours, not days, to read. The procedural critique from Republicans (Sen. Sampson: "I just got this bill. This is yet another strike-all amendment") is the clearest evidence that speed-of-assembly was itself a governance design choice, not incidental. The CT Mirror also makes explicit that this year's approach deliberately shifted from regulating specific *users* (the anti-discrimination frame of 2024-2025) to regulating specific *uses* (AEDT, AI companions, frontier whistleblowing) — a framing strategy that avoided the "business-hostile" characterization that killed earlier bills. Senate Pro Tem Looney's closing line — "History will not look with favor on those who say we should wait" — captures the urgency framing that eventually overcame both Republican opposition and the governor's prior veto threats.

The CBIA article is the most analytically useful of the two for understanding the AEDT provisions. It provides detail absent from the OLR analysis: (1) the effective date for AEDT employer obligations is **October 1, 2027** (not 2026 — different from §1's AI subscription provisions); (2) the AG has discretion to issue cure notices for violations through end of 2027, providing a limited compliance runway; (3) developers and deployers (employers) may contractually allocate compliance obligations, but roles must be defined in writing; (4) trade secret protections are preserved but require the withholding party to identify what is withheld and why; (5) anti-bias testing by employers is a mitigating factor courts may consider but does not eliminate liability. The CBIA frames these as significant compliance burdens; the framing is business-advocacy but the factual detail is accurate and adds precision.

The CBIA article also gives the clearest summary of §15 (Embedded Metadata/Content Provenance), which our earlier processing underweighted: providers with more than one million monthly users must embed content provenance metadata in AI-generated or materially altered images, audio, and video, consistent with emerging technical standards. Business-to-business uses are exempt. Violations are CUTPA violations enforced by the AG only.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

- The CBIA's employer-advocacy framing of the AEDT provisions as creating "significant compliance burden" and "new legal risk" is a useful corrective to the neutrality of the OLR analysis, but note that CBIA opposed the bill — their characterization of scope should be read as maximum-liability advocacy.
- The cure-notice provision (AG discretion through end of 2027) is structurally similar to the compliance runway provisions in GDPR's implementation — it is a common legislative tool for easing transition without giving up enforcement authority.
