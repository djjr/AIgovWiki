## Précis

Globig et al. (NYU / Google, 2025 working paper) investigates whether AI information sources bypass the identity-driven biases that distort how people evaluate human partisan sources. In three studies (n=1,054; 284; 277) using both self-report and drift-diffusion modeling of evidence accumulation, participants preferred AI-generated information over both ingroup and outgroup partisan sources — with the ingroup preference suppression being the more politically significant finding. The mechanism identified is *selective evidence accumulation*: participants weighted AI-sourced evidence more uniformly and less quickly toward identity-confirming conclusions. The paper frames AI's perceived neutrality as a potential epistemic intervention — breaking the selective exposure cycle that amplifies polarization when information is attributed to partisan sources.

The governance implications are double-edged. On the optimistic reading, AI as information source could reduce tribal epistemics and increase epistemic common ground — a rare positive externality for democratic discourse. On the pessimistic reading (reinforced by the companion [[wiki/sources/sycophantic-ai-attitude-extremity|Globig/Rathje sycophancy paper]]), the same perceived neutrality that bypasses source bias also makes sycophantic validation more potent: users who trust AI's "neutral" framing are more susceptible to attitude change when AI agrees with them. Together, the two papers suggest AI epistemic effects are contingent on interaction design, not intrinsic to the technology.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The paper's optimistic finding (AI reduces partisan source bias) must be read against the sycophancy paper's finding (agreeable AI increases extremity). The net epistemic effect of AI on democratic epistemics is an empirical open question, not resolvable by either paper alone. Both papers together make the case that AI epistemic governance — not just capability governance — is a live governance domain.
