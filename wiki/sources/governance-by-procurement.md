## Précis

This Harvard Kennedy School / Carr Center commentary (2026) analyzes the Anthropic-Pentagon standoff of February–March 2026 as a structural illustration of a governance failure, not a story of corporate heroism. When the Department of War threatened to designate Anthropic a supply-chain risk for refusing to remove prohibitions on mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons, CEO Dario Amodei refused. The standoff resolved not through any democratic process or multilateral forum, but through a private executive's unilateral ethical judgment — and within 24 hours, the Pentagon blacklisted Anthropic and signed a rushed deal with OpenAI. The essay's central argument: a governance system in which human rights protections depend on the personal convictions of a technology executive is not a governance system. It is a vacancy.

The essay develops the concept of **governance by procurement**: the most consequential AI governance decisions — what systems may be used for, whose data they may collect, how they may be deployed in conflict — are increasingly made not by democratic institutions or multilateral forums but in contracts between governments and technology companies, without transparency, public accountability, or remedy mechanisms for affected populations. The deepest problem is structural and global: most countries that will be affected by U.S.-contracted AI systems have no seat at the negotiating table. The essay grounds its analysis in the universality principle of the 1948 human rights framework — rights that protect *all people* against arbitrary power — and argues the current bilateral-contract architecture is structurally incompatible with that universality. It points to existing multilateral mechanisms (ITU, ISO/IEC 42001, the Global Digital Compact, the WSIS+20 process) as available but underengaged levers, and closes with the observation that governance vacancies compound: each time a CEO makes a decision the international community has failed to make, the vacancy becomes harder to fill.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

Directly extends the [[wiki/sources/hadfield-democratic-deficit|Hadfield democratic deficit]] argument — Hadfield observes AI is being governed inside companies; this essay specifies the mechanism: bilateral procurement contracts with governments. The "rights vs. discretions" distinction ("rights that depend on the goodwill of a technology executive are not rights; they are discretions") is the sharpest formulation in the wiki of the voluntary governance failure problem, and directly challenges the voluntary-commitment frameworks analyzed in [[wiki/sources/anthropic-rsp|Anthropic RSP]], [[wiki/sources/openai-preparedness-framework|OpenAI Preparedness]], and [[wiki/sources/gdm-frontier-safety-framework|GDM Frontier Safety]]. The King's College nuclear crisis study cited (GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 3 Flash in simulated nuclear scenarios — 95% nuclear signaling, zero de-escalation) adds empirical texture to the agentic security risk documented in [[wiki/sources/securing-agentic-ai|FAR AI agentic security]].
