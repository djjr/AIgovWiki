# Responsible AI Governance — Review and Research Framework (Papagiannidis, Mikalef & Conboy, 2024)

## Précis

Published in the *Journal of Strategic Information Systems* (2024), this systematic literature review synthesizes empirical research on responsible AI from an information systems and management perspective. The paper's primary contribution is conceptual: it differentiates between *responsible AI principles* (normative values specifying what AI should be) and *responsible AI governance* (the organizational practices by which those principles are actually implemented), arguing that this distinction has been undertheorized and that the governance side is where most practical failure occurs.

The paper identifies seven responsible AI principles synthesized from EU and OECD frameworks: accountability, diversity/non-discrimination/fairness, human agency and oversight, privacy and data governance, technical robustness and safety, transparency, and social and environmental well-being. It then proposes a framework for responsible AI governance organized around three practice types — *structural* (authority, roles, governance bodies), *procedural* (lifecycle processes, audit, compliance mechanisms), and *relational* (stakeholder engagement, AI literacy, external partnerships) — situated within a broader context of antecedents (societal norms, organizational values, stated RAI principles) and effects (business value, social assessment). The central finding: organizations systematically deprioritize RAI principles during actual implementation, and the missing link is not better principles but better governance practices.

The IS framing distinguishes this paper from the policy documents and technical safety frameworks that dominate the rest of this wiki. It asks how organizations actually enact responsibility rather than what regulations require or what technical tools can assess. The research agenda is specific: how do path dependencies filter normative signals? How should organizations scale RAI governance across levels? What processes enable auditability? How does legal mandate (EU AI Act) interact with prior organizational culture?

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The paper's IS perspective surfaces a tension with the [[wiki/definitions/regulatory-markets|Regulatory Markets]] and [[wiki/definitions/independent-verification-organization|IVO]] framework: the three governance practice types (structural/procedural/relational) are the organizational capabilities that an AI developer would need to have in place before IVO certification would be meaningful. An IVO auditing a developer whose governance practices are purely formal — structural roles without genuine authority, procedural checklists without institutional backing, relational engagement as theater — would face the same illegibility problem Hadfield identifies for conventional regulators. The paper therefore implies that organizational governance capacity is a prerequisite for the Regulatory Markets proposal to function.

The paper's conclusions also note the EU AI Act as creating legally mandated structural and procedural requirements for high-risk AI operators — making the principle-to-practice gap, for some operators, a compliance problem rather than a voluntary-governance problem. Whether mandate closes the gap or produces sophisticated compliance theater is explicitly flagged as an empirical open question.
