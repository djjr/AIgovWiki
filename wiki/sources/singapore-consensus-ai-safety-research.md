## Précis

The Singapore Consensus (Bengio et al., 2025), co-organized by SCAI and IMDA and signed by 100+ researchers from 11 countries, is the first broad-based international research consensus document on AI safety priorities. It emerged from the Singapore AI Safety Summit (May 2025) and is explicitly framed as identifying "areas of mutual interest" where even commercially and geopolitically competing AI developers should cooperate. The document organizes the field into three research pillars: **Risk Assessment** (understanding what can go wrong — evaluation techniques, benchmarks, downstream impact assessment, secure evaluation infrastructure, system safety, metrology, dangerous capability assessment, and loss-of-control assessment); **Development** (making AI safer by design — specification and validation, robustness, formal verification); and **Control** (ensuring human oversight persists — AI system monitoring, AI ecosystem monitoring, societal resilience). The framing as defence-in-depth is explicit: no single layer is sufficient.

The document's governance significance is substantial. It functions as a legitimation device for the [[wiki/definitions/technical-ai-governance|TAIG]] field, translating a research agenda previously articulated in academic form (Reuel et al. 2024) into a multilateral consensus backed by 33 governments via IAISR. Its emphasis on "areas of mutual interest" — particularly verification mechanisms and risk thresholds — directly operationalizes the TAIG finding that technical tools precede policy, since no international agreement on AI risk can function without shared evaluation infrastructure. The consensus also provides a politically workable framing: by identifying cooperation on measurement as prior to and independent of disagreements about regulation, it carves out a non-zero-sum space for adversarial AI powers to collaborate on safety science.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

Broadly consistent with [[wiki/sources/open-problems-technical-ai-governance|Open Problems in TAIG]] but operates at a higher level of abstraction — TAIG lists specific tractable problems; the Singapore Consensus identifies priority areas. The overlap is significant (evaluation infrastructure, dangerous capability assessment, monitoring) but the Singapore Consensus is notably weaker on governance mechanisms (IVOs, reporting requirements) and stronger on societal resilience. The Caputo scrutability framework is implicit but not named. The document's optimism about cooperative verification contrasts with [[wiki/findings/frontier-model-transparency-declining|declining frontier model transparency]] — the empirical trend runs against the normative program.
