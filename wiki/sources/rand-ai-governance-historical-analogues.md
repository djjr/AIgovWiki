# RAND — Historical Analogues That Can Inform AI Governance (Vermeer, 2024)

## Précis

Michael Vermeer (RAND, 2024) surveys four earlier technology governance episodes — nuclear technology, the Internet, encryption products, and genetic engineering — and extracts structural lessons for AI governance. The organizing argument is that no single analogy maps cleanly onto AI, but each illuminates specific governance challenges that will apply to some category of AI application depending on how AI's characteristics evolve: resource requirements, physical vs. nonphysical asset structure, security risk consensus, and dual-use character.

Three cross-cutting themes emerge from the four cases. First, sustained consensus on norms is the critical variable that distinguishes governance successes from failures — not institutional design alone. Nuclear governance and the first Asilomar moratorium (recombinant DNA, 1975) succeeded because stakeholders agreed on what risk they were managing; encryption governance collapsed when that consensus fractured between government and private sector. Second, the governance of nonphysical assets — software, algorithms, model weights — is structurally harder than governing physical ones. Export controls on encryption code were ultimately defeated by First Amendment challenges and open-source dissemination; the same pattern threatens AI model weight controls. Third, public-private partnerships in which private communities voluntarily develop and enforce norms (as with Internet standards bodies and the Asilomar scientific community) have outperformed top-down mandates in domains with rapidly evolving technology and distributed development.

The report maps AI against three scenarios. If AI has high resource barriers, controllable physical assets, and clear catastrophic-risk consensus, elements of the nuclear/IAEA model apply. If AI poses minimal safety risks, Internet-style governance with public-private norm communities is appropriate. If AI has low barriers to entry but serious risks, the encryption history is a cautionary tale and genetic engineering governance (WHO/National Academies as norm-synthesizing bodies) is the better model. The report concludes with appropriate epistemic humility: "AI is like all these examples, and none of them."

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The report's encryption cautionary tale bears on the [[wiki/findings/compute-threshold-as-governance-proxy|Compute Thresholds as Governance Proxy]] finding: if the relevant AI assets (model weights, training algorithms) are nonphysical, compute hardware controls may share encryption export controls' structural weakness — motivated actors can route around them. The nuclear comparison also complicates the [[wiki/findings/converging-risk-domains-bio-cyber-autonomy|Convergence on Priority Risk Domains]] finding: Vermeer argues that AI catastrophic risk scenarios currently lack the concreteness and public salience of nuclear scenarios, which may limit the kind of norm consensus that made nuclear governance possible. The Internet analogy speaks directly to the [[wiki/findings/broad-adoption-shallow-commitment-dynamic|OECD Principles dynamic]]: Internet governance norms worked because developers shared them organically; broad adoption of principles by non-adherents doesn't substitute for internalized norms.
