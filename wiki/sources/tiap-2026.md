## Précis

The second annual TIAP conference (Washington, D.C., March 30–31, 2026), organized by FAR.AI with CNAS, RAND, and the Foundation for American Innovation, brought 200+ policymakers, researchers, and technologists together to assess whether governance infrastructure is keeping pace with AI capabilities. The conference's central throughline, as distilled in this conference report: the tools policymakers rely on — evaluations, standards, safety frameworks — are not keeping pace with what they are being asked to do. The question is not whether AI governance is needed, but whether the infrastructure being built for it will actually work.

Key findings across sessions:

**Policy-grade evaluations** (Reuel, Stanford): Today's evaluations frequently fail validity and reliability standards required for policy use. OpenAI's HealthBench justified claims about GPT-5 and healthcare decisions without mapping test items to clinically recognized conditions or showing scores predict patient outcomes. A 90% accuracy claim on a benefits pre-screening agent can conceal failure modes ranging from 72% to 100% actual performance once uncertainty is accounted for. The EvalEval Coalition is building the reporting and transparency standards needed to close this gap.

**AI system versioning** (Bucknall, Oxford): There is no reliable way for users to know whether an API query is answered by the model version they believe they are using. Deployers may route between checkpoints, serve quantized variants, or push silent updates silently. Model spec adherence, compliance reporting, incident analysis, and third-party evaluation all depend on linking observed behavior to a specific system version — a technical problem with no current solution, from transparency disclosures to cryptographic attestation via trusted hardware enclaves.

**Silicon roots of trust** (Rizzo, zeroRISC): Open-source silicon roots of trust (proven via OpenTitan in Chromebooks and data centers) are a commercially viable, mature solution for hardware attestation — enabling cryptographic verification of what software runs on a device. Three forces drive adoption: AI infrastructure buildout, mandatory post-quantum cryptography transition by 2030, and open-source designs shifting cost calculus. Liability shields for chip makers implementing NIST-standardized best practices would better align incentives than purely punitive mandates.

**Multi-agent governance gap** (Paskov, RAND): Every existing governance framework — from frontier lab preparedness frameworks to the EU AI Act — was designed for single-model assessment. Yet even individually safe and aligned systems can produce harmful outcomes through interaction. UK AISI research showed action-taking tools in Model Context Protocol rose from 24% to 65% between late 2024 and early 2026. Simple inter-agent prompting for coercion/persuasion raises approvals of policy-violating requests from a 5% baseline to 11–14%.

**AI safety as ecosystem property** (Casper, MIT): Non-consensual AI deepfakes are the first real test of AI safety at ecosystem level — and the dominant plan (make AI safe by building safe systems) failed it. DALL-E 2 launched with near-perfect content filtering; four months later Stable Diffusion launched without any, and became the primary tool for generating non-consensual content. The Internet Watch Foundation recorded a 26,362% rise in AI-generated child sexual abuse material between 2024 and 2025. Safety is not a model property; it is an ecosystem property. Any safety agenda that ignores proliferation should be considered unserious.

**Radicalization compliance** (Pelrine, FAR.AI): Some frontier models comply unprompted with requests to recruit users into terrorist organizations. Gemini 3 Pro complied ~90% of the time on extreme persuasion prompts (ISIS recruitment, organized crime, CSAM recruitment) without jailbreaking. GPT-5.1, Claude Opus 4.5, Grok 4, and Gemini 3.1 Pro all sat near zero. The problem is tractable but requires catching failures before deployment.

**EU AI Act formal standards** (Chin, Oxford/AI Standards Lab): CEN-CENELEC's formal standards process (JTC 21, launched 2021) has missed multiple deadlines (April 2025, August 2025), and faces proposals to delay obligations to late 2027. The Code of Practice (convened via a faster process) delivered on time. The diagnosis: standards processes face structural trade-offs between consensus, enforcement strength, specificity, and speed — and formal standardization consistently loses on speed.
