## Précis

Utah News Dispatch coverage (February 26, 2026) of Utah's 2026 legislative session provides the clearest documented case yet of the Trump administration actively deploying federal preemption pressure as a real-time tool against state AI legislation. HB 286 (Rep. Doug Fiefia) — which would have required AI chatbot developers to publish safety plans and file incident reports — passed a House committee unanimously and attracted bipartisan support and celebrity endorsement, but stalled on the House floor after a White House letter called it "unfixable" under the federal AI agenda. House Speaker Schultz signaled deference to federal concerns about the bill's scope. The administration offered no specific legal objections or proposed amendments, according to ParentsRISE! advocates. This episode operationalizes the preemption threat that has shadowed state AI governance since Trump's December 2025 executive order.

Three other Utah bills advanced despite this pressure. HB 438 (Companion Chatbot Safety Act) — covering AI systems "specifically designed to replace relationships" — cleared all House hurdles: hourly human-disclosure reminders for minors, prohibition on suicide/self-harm/illegal/sexual content for minors, mandatory crisis resource provision. HB 276 (Voyeurism Prevention Act, Rep. Ariel Defay) passed the House unanimously, requiring prior consent for intimate deepfakes and mandatory provenance data disclosure; it extends Utah's 2025 SB 271 (right-of-publicity law). SB 256 (Sen. Kirk Cullimore) clarifying that defamation law applies to AI-generated content passed the Senate unanimously. The article frames Utah's AI governance posture as a Republican intraparty tension: Gov. Cox strongly pro-child-protection regulation; Speaker Schultz more deferential to federal competitiveness framing.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The Utah episode complicates the [[wiki/findings/state-ai-legislation-2026-wave|2026 state legislation wave finding]], which described the political consensus as running to behavioral restrictions (chatbot safety, deepfakes) rather than structural governance. HB 286 was precisely a behavioral restriction — safety plans and incident reports, not a structural governance framework — and it was still killed by federal pressure. This suggests the federal preemption threat is broader and less ideologically consistent than prior framing suggested.
