## Précis

Caputo (Oxford Martin School, 2026 working draft) identifies a structural tension — the *capability-accountability trap* — running through American administrative law since the Interstate Commerce Commission Act of 1887: to gain the expertise needed to regulate complex technical domains, regulatory agencies must delegate to specialists, but delegation reduces the cognitive tractability of those decisions for political overseers, courts, and the public. Caputo operationalizes this through Herbert Simon's bounded rationality framework, introducing *scrutability* as the key variable — the degree to which a regulatory decision is cognitively tractable to an authorized overseer. The trap closes when regulators acquire the capability to handle complex technical problems but lose scrutability in doing so; alternatively, when courts or Congress restore scrutability by constraining agency action (as in the recent SCOTUS trilogy: *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, *West Virginia v. EPA*, *SEC v. Jarkesy*), capability is sacrificed.

The paper's central claim for AI governance is original and important: AI systems can serve as *scrutability infrastructure* — tools that make complex technical regulatory decisions interpretable to non-specialist overseers, thereby allowing capability and accountability to coexist rather than trade off. This reframes AI governance not only as a problem of regulating AI but as an opportunity to use AI to repair administrative law's long-standing structural deficit. The argument connects to [[wiki/definitions/technical-legibility|Technical Legibility]] (the property IVOs need to audit AI systems) and implies that legibility tools benefit not just regulators but the constitutional structure of agency oversight. Caputo also draws a direct analogy to the RAND historical analogues cluster: each technology that escaped the capability-accountability trap did so through institutional design, not just technical capability.

## Tensions / contradictions with existing wiki

The paper's framing partially inverts the governance-technical direction of [[wiki/sources/open-problems-technical-ai-governance|Open Problems in TAIG]]: where TAIG asks what technical tools governance needs, Caputo asks how governance institutions must be redesigned to absorb technical complexity. Both diagnoses are compatible; neither resolves the other. Also in tension with [[wiki/findings/government-technical-capacity-as-governance-prerequisite|Government Technical Capacity as Governance Prerequisite]] — Caputo's argument is that raw technical capacity is insufficient; what matters is whether that capacity is scrutably exercised.
