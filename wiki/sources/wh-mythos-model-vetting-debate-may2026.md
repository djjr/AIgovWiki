## Précis

These two articles — an NYT piece by Mickle, Barnes, Frenkel and Volz (May 4, 2026) and a Politico piece by Sakellariadis et al. (May 7, 2026) — document a three-day policy whiplash inside the Trump White House that reveals genuine internal tension over AI governance. The precipitating event is Anthropic's announcement of Claude Mythos Preview: a cybersecurity model Anthropic judged too powerful to release publicly, sharing it instead with a limited number of tech and security organizations to get ahead of vulnerability exploitation. This "Mythos moment" triggered real concern inside the White House about being caught flat-footed if a more powerful model caused a catastrophic cyberattack or enabled adversary exploitation.

The NYT piece (May 4) reports that the administration was actively discussing an executive order to create a working group of tech executives and government officials to examine oversight procedures for new AI models, potentially including a formal pre-release government review process analogous to the UK's model safety evaluation framework. White House officials had briefed Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI executives on these plans. Leadership context: David Sacks (AI czar) left in March; Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Bessent stepped into the AI policy role, signaling a harder-edged approach than Sacks's deregulatory instincts.

The Politico piece (May 7, three days later) shows the White House pulling back hard after NEC Director Kevin Hassett's Fox Business interview compared the potential pre-release review to FDA drug approval — a comment that "unsettled people in industry circles." Senior White House officials walked back Hassett's remarks, saying the administration was seeking "partnership" not "government regulation." Chief of Staff Susie Wiles issued a rare tweet ("not in the business of picking winners and losers") signaling the White House's primary goal was to soothe industry anxiety rather than announce new regulation. Meanwhile, CAISI (Center for AI Standards and Innovation) announced voluntary safety-testing agreements with Google DeepMind, xAI, and Microsoft; OpenAI simultaneously announced a limited preview of GPT-5.5-Cyber.

Running beneath both pieces is the Anthropic-Pentagon standoff: DOD designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk" in March after the company refused to allow its models in autonomous lethal attacks and mass surveillance; Trump gave federal agencies six months to stop using Anthropic products; Anthropic sued. Yet federal agencies — including NSA, which was using Mythos to assess government software vulnerabilities — desperately wanted access back. The dispute makes any coherent government AI model governance policy harder to develop, since it entangles capability questions with a live legal conflict.

The pair of articles together illustrates the "Mythos moment" as a structural turning point: even the most deregulatory US administration since Reagan found itself internally divided over frontier AI oversight when confronted with a concrete capability that exceeded existing governance frameworks.
